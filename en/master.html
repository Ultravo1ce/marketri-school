Tatiana Gorina

Marquetry master and teacher. Studio on Broshevsky Lane in Moscow.

I picked up a cutting knife at the age of forty.

How I came to marquetry

I tell everyone that I came to marquetry by accident. I had no special training at all: I’m a seamstress by profession, I sewed, knitted and dug vegetable beds. Then my brother, a cabinetmaker, started teaching his craft to his stepdaughter. I watched them and asked him: “Why don’t you teach me too?” That’s how it all began.

At first I practised on simple things: little dogs, coats of arms, flowers. I made my first work with real volume after Salvador Dalí, who inspired me a great deal back then.

Then my brother broke his arm and needed help, so I went to work for him as a marquetry maker. This time it was real commissioned pieces. Some time later he closed the workshop, and I moved to the restoration workshops on Chistye Prudy. I worked there for a very long time.

I have no art education either. I think I simply have a knack for it. As a child I loved appliqué, and that’s almost the same thing: you cut out and you glue, only the technique is a little different.

Works and commissions

In my time I did a lot of commissions, for ambassadors and for industrial magnates. I made many “shirts” for furniture, the inlaid veneer coverings. I even bought an industrial laser so as not to wear my hands out on repeating details: ornaments, frames, monograms.

I’m inspired by nature, and I love conveying it in wood. It’s hard: you have to work really hard to show volume. I redid the pear in one still life five times before I got a result I was happy with.

Some masters don’t like bright colours that are unnatural for wood and work only with stained veneer. I do the opposite: I dye veneer and darken it with hot sand. In short, I use every technique there is.

About veneer

Marquetry can’t be cheap by definition. First, it’s a long and complicated process. Second, the wood species we use are mostly expensive. And to work successfully, you need a large collection of material.

I probably have the largest collection of veneer there is, nobody else has one like it. I keep it in plastic containers: that’s the proper way to store it. People ask me how I remember so many different species. I tell them: once you buy it yourself, you’ll remember too. Wood passes not only through your hands, but through your pockets as well.

How I teach

These days I make few works myself and mostly teach. We work together with my students, and they always have different requests, so I have to study every style. Sometimes they leave me stumped: they see things differently, not the way I do. I had never looked at things from that side, but they do. That’s why our work is a shared creative process: I learn from them, and they learn from me.

We used to buy veneer for several thousand dollars at a time. Now I teach differently: once you have an idea for a work, go to the shop with your sketch and choose the veneer to suit it.
