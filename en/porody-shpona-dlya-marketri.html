Veneer species for marquetry: colours, texture and how to choose

Tatiana Gorina School of Marquetry, 9 November 2026

Marquetry uses no paint: the whole palette comes from the wood species. Here are the veneers masters use most often and how to combine them.

Light species

Light veneer is used for backgrounds, skies, highlights and pale details.

  • Maple — almost white, with a fine even texture. Bird’s-eye maple, with its figure of tiny “eyes”, is especially prized.
  • Birch — light, with a warm tint. Karelian birch, with its marbled figure, is a classic of Russian furniture.
  • Ash — light, with pronounced grain stripes. Good where texture is needed: water, fabric, clouds.

Mid tones

  • Pear — pinkish brown, dense and even. It is loved because it cuts easily and does not split, which makes pear good for small details.
  • Oak — golden brown, with a bold grain.
  • Cherry — warm and reddish. In the light it gradually darkens and deepens.

Dark species

  • Walnut — from greyish brown to chocolate. Suits shadows, tree trunks, outlines.
  • Bog oak — almost black. This is oak that lay in water for a long time and darkened naturally.
  • Wenge — dark brown with almost black stripes.
  • Rosewood — dark, with a purple or reddish tint.

Coloured and exotic species

Some species give colours you would never expect from wood:

  • padauk — bright red, turning reddish brown over time;
  • purpleheart — purple, browning in the light;
  • zebrano — light with contrasting dark stripes.

Besides natural species, masters use dyed veneer: wood coloured right through. It gives green, blue and other colours that do not occur in nature.

Burls and the cut

The figure of veneer depends not only on the species but also on how the log was cut. A quarter cut gives straight parallel stripes, a flat cut gives wavy “cathedrals”. Burls, the growths on a trunk, give a complex swirling figure. They are used to convey foliage, stone and clouds.

How to choose veneer for your work

Think in contrasts. Neighbouring pieces should differ in tone, otherwise the picture will blur. It is easy to check: squint and look at the pieces you have chosen.

Use the grain. Grain direction works like a brushstroke: vertical stripes for tree trunks and folds of fabric, wavy ones for water.

Allow for time. Many species change colour in the light. Cherry darkens, padauk and purpleheart turn brown, light species yellow. We explain how to keep the colours for a long time in the article on caring for marquetry.
