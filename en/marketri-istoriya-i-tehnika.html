Marquetry: the history and technique of wood inlay

Tatiana Gorina School of Marquetry, 9 November 2026

Marquetry is one of the most delicate techniques of decorative woodwork. Here is where it came from, how it works and how it differs from similar crafts.

What is marquetry?

Marquetry is a picture or an ornament assembled from pieces of veneer of different wood species and glued onto a backing board. Veneer is a thin sheet of wood, usually 0.6–0.8 mm thick.

The colour in such a work comes not from paint but from the wood itself: light maple and birch, golden oak, reddish cherry, dark walnut. The grain works like a brushstroke, conveying the shimmer of water, the folds of fabric or the texture of foliage.

The word comes from French: marqueterie derives from the verb marqueter, “to mark, to variegate”.

Where the technique comes from

Decorating objects with insets of wood, bone and stone was already known in ancient Egypt. In Renaissance Italy, intarsia flourished: inlaid wooden pictures on the walls of studies and the backs of church stalls. The best-known example is the studiolo of Duke Federico da Montefeltro in Urbino.

Marquetry reached its height in 17th and 18th century France. The cabinetmaker André-Charles Boulle became famous for his inlays of brass and tortoiseshell. In the 18th century, Jean-François Oeben and Jean-Henri Riesener created furniture for the royal court with floral and landscape designs in dozens of wood species.

In Russia, inlaid wood was prized in the 18th and 19th centuries, when it adorned palace furniture and parquet floors. Famous inlaid floors survive, for example, in the Ostankino Palace. A new wave of interest came with Art Nouveau, when Émile Gallé and the masters of the School of Nancy turned to marquetry.

How a work is made

  1. Sketch. The drawing is traced onto tracing paper and divided into pieces, each of which becomes a separate part.
  2. Choosing the veneer. A species is chosen for each piece by colour, texture and grain direction.
  3. Cutting. The pieces are cut with a knife or a fine fretsaw. In packet cutting, several sheets of different species are cut at once, so the pieces match their outlines exactly.
  4. Assembly. The pieces are fitted together on the face side and held with paper tape.
  5. Gluing and finishing. The design is glued onto a backing board under a press, then sanded and coated with oil or varnish.

Techniques that create depth

Sand shading. The edge of a piece is dipped into hot sand and it darkens. This gives volume and shadow without any dyes.

Grain direction. The same veneer turned at different angles reflects light differently. Masters use this to bring out details of the image.

Marquetry, intarsia and parquetry

  • Intarsia — pieces are set into recesses cut in solid wood.
  • Marquetry — the whole picture is assembled from veneer and glued onto a backing board.
  • Parquetry — the same principle, but with a geometric pattern: cubes, diamonds, a chequerboard.

Today the words “marquetry” and “intarsia” are often used as synonyms.

Where marquetry is used today

The technique decorates panels, boxes, chessboards, tabletops, cabinet doors and the walls of studies. It is one-of-a-kind work: a single panel takes a master weeks.
